# Crash and Burn

I don't think I'm alone in feeling like my life has a soundtrack, a lot of which is out of my control. As much as I try to curate the sounds I am surrounded by, a lot of stuff that I wouldn't choose gets through and finds a salience in my little brain that triggers nearly a nearly disembodying sense of place and time. As much as I know that I don't want to be a Dave Matthews fan, you couldn't walk across my college campus in the Fall of '94 without hearing "Ants Marching" echoing out the windows of nearly every dorm you passed. He came up again on a night home from school a few years later with the release of their next album. My best friend from highschool, Dave, was in town, and we were driving to a little get-together that would be a memorable night. Dave had a habit of getting sucked into certain albums and it was infectious the way he made you believe it was the best thing around. This happened to us my senior year. Counting Crows "August and Everything After" is a funny one. First, he was obsessed with it but it was also one of the albums in the 5-disc changer at a restaurant I worked at. It usually came on early in the evening but I mostly remember hearing "'Round Here" late at night on its second rotation while I was stacking chairs and cleaning up. It was a bummer of a song and tracked with my feeling that I was going nowhere. I was supposed to head off to the aforementioned college in the Fall, but my grades my last semester were less than stellar. I got a letter from them in the mail asking me to offer an explaination. I never responded. I didn't know how, or I was overwhelmed with fear, or whatever but I didn't so I assumed I was going to get a subsequent letter telling me to fuck off and I was going to spend the rest of my life bussing tables at Bobby's Bistro. Meanwhile, Dave would blare "Mr. Jones" and rock out in the car and we'd have a blast. He did this with Green Day's "Dookie" as well. You'll notice these were all very popular albums. It really ruined his street cred but it was infectious the way he made you believe him. He'd do it in subsequent years with other albums and he actually got me and my now wife to come out to Coachella the year of the indie bands. He was usually right. Show the guy some up-and-coming Brit-pop frontman in a pair of yellow-colored sunglasses and he was in. I fell for it everytime, and most of them stuck. Stone Roses, Oasis, etc. I have since started seeing his endorsement as the beginning of the end of that band's obscurity. And good for both of them, but I still have trouble shaking my Gen-X DNA that says "popular==lame." 
Much earlier than that, though, my earliest memories are in my Aunt Cricket's car. She was a tennis player, only 16 when I was born, and drove Firebirds and Trans-Ams with t-tops.  This was late 70's and early 80's so it was mostly Michael Jackson. But Earth, Wind, and Fire, Steely Dan, and Kenny Loggins made the cut. Later, when we spent time with her and her always much older male cohorts, it was Chuck Berry and Jerry Lee Lewis. But when I was little, she'd always bang on the gear shift and make me terrified that she going to accidentally send the care into reverse and throw us through the windshield.
I was always obsessed with music -- just as a concept. My mother would take us to Peaches and let me spend an outrageous amount of money on tapes. Until about 7th grade, it was all pop music - whatever I heard on the Top Five at Nine. There was something I liked about owning it, having my own copy. We didn't have the instant access to hearing whatever you wanted. If you wanted to hear a song, you have to hope it was popular enough to be in rotation on the radio, be in a place with a jukebox, or have your own copy. I would become obsessed with songs , at least and more likely, with the feelings they evoked. That may have been more important at the time than I was aware of. Parts of my childhood were a little tumultuous and unpredicatble and the predicatability of my emotional response to something was probably very important.
