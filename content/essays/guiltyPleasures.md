The blanket acceptance *guilty pleasure* to excuse our indulgence of things that aren't collectively considered cool, classy, or otherwise justifiable is an efficient indicator of how necessary these transgressions of taste are. The longer you're alive, or, rather, the more experiences you cobble together, the less control you have over the things that hold meaning for you and, thus, become indulgences. Only from the safe, sterile perch of the critic's desk can one maintain a proper set of standards for art and food and music and whatever else. As I get older, though, I am beginning to wonder if these *guilty* ones have the most meat. They end up being the little splashes of color that decorate the brutalist, non-descript cubicle that your life can become when you're not paying attention. *IF* you're thoughtful, you start to find that your tastes are arguably *more* refined than the so-called critic's - that lived experience trumps the academic every time.

The silliness of critique is well-illustrated by Mr. Keating in his first interaction with students in *Dead Poets Society*.  <img src="\img\keating.jpeg" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 300px;"> Of course, I saw this moving as a young man who was looking for a mentor so it hit well. My understanding is that it hasn't aged well &mdash; trigger warnings and all. At any rate, young Neil Perry reads from the introduction to their poetry text as written by Dr. J. Evan Pritchard, PhD. He explains that we can evaluate a poem by scoring it on a Cartesian plane with *Perfection* on the \(x\)-axis and *Importance* on the \(y\). We can thus determine the poem's greatness by it's total area. Mr. Keating calls this "excrement" and orders these buttoned-up little foundlings to tear out the page &mdash; no, tear out the whole introduction &mdash; from their pristine text books which they are likely to be fined for come the end of the semester.

The lesson here is more layered than we give it credit for. This is a lesson in interrogating the sacred. Both the completeness of the book and the authority of the voice therein are categorically dismissed. Such a lesson is a risky one to give to young men (I can only speak as having been a young man though I expect it is not limited by sex or gender). Now, authority is going to come under question eventually. At least one of a group will be stricken awake one late night when they say to themselves *wait a second, why are we doing this? why is that even a thing?* I specify the *one* because it isn't always clear that you're abiding by totally arbitrary rules and conventions and there is little likelihood that someone is going to point it out to you. It takes a confluence of circumstances to make this salient and if you're lucky enough to have a few friends with varying degrees of trauma going on, one of them is bound to stumble uponm the realization and want to share it.
Even in middle age, I find myself wandering around just doing stuff I'm supposed to. A couple of years ago, I was (uncharacteristically) helping my wife make the bed. I asked her how she knew which direction the fitted sheet went on the bed. "There's was a little tag in one of the corners and it goes on the top left." Exasperated by the notion that it involved investigation at all, I thought a sec and responded: "Why don't we just take a marker and draw people lying in the bed &mdash; I mean they're *our* fucking sheets." We laughed a little more than normal people would but, *yes, they ARE our goddamn sheets and we can do whatever we want to them*. This is not insignificant: two people that have made a life together, whose course has been not insignificantly guided by the precept "go fuck yourself" when it comes to an authority flex,having to *realize* that they are oppressing themselves.

Of course, a lot of things we're supposed to do, *we're supposed to do*. The idea that just because something is a rule or convention, it is bad is moronic. This is where the youth run into trouble.  John Lydon made this mistake recently when he deemed it punk to be right-wing simply because it was unpopular with the masses. You can see {placeholder} for more on rules and arbitrary authority.
We're talking about taste and subjectivity.  <img src="\img\daveMatthews.jpg" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 150px;">I'll start with this: I don't want to like the Dave Matthews Band &mdash; it doesn't gel with my whole schtick. However, I went to college on the Fall of 1994 in the SouthEast. You could not walk by a dorm without *Ants Marching* blaring at different parts of the song from several windows. The next two years would be the same with Hootie and the Blowfish and Rusted Root and, often, with the cooler hippies, Widespread Panic. 

Now, I radicalized myself as a punk in like 1991. I heard Social Distortion while I was hanging out with my cousin and we got into a whole thing. The next day, <img src="\img\c-n-b.webp" style="float: right; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 200px;"> I picked up copies of Minor Threat *Out of Step*, the Misfits *Legacy of Brutality*, and Black Flag *Damaged* and committed to the bit. I had set foot in Crash-n-Burn (See {}) and the inhaling the assaulting scent of the leather boots and silk-screen solvents changed my DNA. I leaned in and went for foundational stuff and became obsessed with the Sex Pistols and the Dead Kennedys. This is all to say that I was not about to allow myself to listen the whimsy of a South African hippy with an acoustic guitar. (There were concessions made for the sake of getting girls like the Indigo Girls and Ani DIfranco &mdash; though she is as punk as they come so it wasn't difficult. And I'm not gonna lie, *Send Me On My Way* and that Blues Traveler song are fucking catchy &mdash; I loved them. They were *in sutu* guilty pleasures.) ### Now, 30+ years later, 
I have no control over the fact that Dave Matthews triggers a sense of fond nostalgia. One night, those many years ago, I tried to control this effect by putting a James Taylor song on repeat for the entirety of a mushroom and wine-fueled evening of madness. To a degree it worked, in that I know what song it was, but it doesn't *take me there* like I was intending. Perhaps this has more to do with the chemistry of the affair or the number of suicidal braincells that didn't survive my alcoholism. However, to keep with my hypothesis, I have no control over all the terrible music of the late 90's that was incessant in the restaurant I worked at, the songs on the radio when I was mostly drunk between classes at USF, which *do*, in fact, *take me there*. I hear Some movies are great at this. *Lost in Translation* overrides any other memory I had of *Just Like *Honey*; Fight Club owns *Where is My Mind*; Resevoir Dogs and *Stuck in the Middle.*

But music is obviously subjective and personal. What about art and food and movies? I have a very priveleged palate that has been around the block but, if I'm honest, the best foods are pizza, hot dogs, chicken tenders, and vanilla ice cream. You can spend hours on your beef wellington and I'll gobble it up as the heavenly treat it is, but it is unlikely to hit the mark any time day or night like a plate of microwved Tostitos and cheddar.

Here it is:
If you like *Grease$$_1$$*, then you have no business trashing *Grease 2*.  Michelle Pfieffer is smokin' hot and *Score Tonight* is a banger. Drowning Mona is one of the best comedies ever. Skinamarink is self-indulgent garbage. I will stick by all these statements and be unmoved by your reasoning to the contrary. However, the approach to defending them are wildly different. Grease 2 is a musical &mdash; they're all silly and criticizing them is a fool's errand. You can like the cast, you can like the songs, you can like the story &mdash; there are depressingly few that are going to nail all three. Drowning Mona? Banger cast, dark and hilariously absurd, and littered with one-liners that alone justify it. ### I love stories about the Gulag
 and imprisonment and actual isolation &mdash; I am totally enthralled with the atmosphere. I have also written quite a bit about the isolation that is a fundamental part of addiction so it speaks to me in a very specific way. However, I do *not* think it is a necessarily fundamental human experience. And that is the crux of all of this. <img src="\img\gulag.jpg" style="float: left; margin-right: 20px; margin-bottom: 20px; max-width: 350px;"> That is probably why I *don't* like movies like *Skinamarink* and *Backrooms* &mdash;  they are too on pointor maybe trying to hard to convey it accurately. I prefer the idea that you can trudge through actual prison labor. That is far more tolerable than sitting in the hall of mirrors and labrythian dead-ends that existential isolation is. The metaphor as a filter or a masking to be translated and evaluated as a survivable circumstance is one that my brain can handle. <img src="\img\skin.webp" style="float: right; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 250px;">I have *been* to jail. I can handle that. An earnest depiction of being adrift in a interminable metaphysical ocean of nothingness is both superfluous and overwhelming &mdash; *for me.*

One the most useful things that came out of the *lived-experience/woke/radical subjectviity* of the last fifteen years is that has allowed for things to exist and be marketable and that you are exposed to that *aren't for you*. Maybe there is something to sitting uncomfortably for 2+ hours of dark and ominous and otherwise disconcerting camera play. That makes sense. And some people can, could, and probably *did* benefit from it. I do think I'd like to see it what would happen if it were projected on the lid of a floating sensory deprivation tank. I bet that would fuck me up real good.

The point is that it wasn't made for me or I didn't find value in the experience it evoked. And that's exactly why we have to be careful about athoritative claims. You can't tell me that I have to like this movie when it is clear that it has a very specific experience that it wants to tap into.  This is different from the syllogistic reasoning of "You don't *have* to like *Grease 2*, but if you're going to bat for *Grease\(_1\)*, then you'd better show up for *Cool Rider*." 

## When I was in my twenties, 
<img src="\img\tmpaTheater.jpeg" style="float: left; margin-right: 20px; margin-bottom: 20px; max-width: 300px;">we used to go to the Tampa Theater for their Summer Classic Movie Series. Booze, friends, and a matinee after brunch was pretty good living. The overture was played live of the Mighty Wurlitzer organ before the shows and the shows were generally unequivocal classics. Some weren't great but I guess were good by association &mdash; like *Spellbound*, but most you'd stick by: *Psycho*, *Casablanca*, *Citizen Kane*. Before *Casablanca* I was talking to my *now*-wife who I was not at all with at the time. She was highly skeptical that this movie could *possibly* live up to its reputation. She was wrong and we had the exact same set of dialogs about *Citizen Kane* when it was shown. I don't want to get into an analysis of films here, but there is something different about beloved movies and critically acclaimed movies. Often they overlap, but we are seeing that less and less.
