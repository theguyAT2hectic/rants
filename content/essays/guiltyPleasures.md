The blanket acceptance of using the term 'guilty pleasure' to excuse our indulgence of things that aren't collectively considered cool, classy, or otherwise justifiable is an efficient indicator of how necessary these transgressions of taste are. The longer you're alive, or, rather, the more experiences you cobble together, the less control you have over the things that hold meaning for you and, thus, become indulgences. Only from the safe, sterile perch of the critic's desk can one maintain a proper set of standards for art and food and music or anything else. As I get older, though, I am beginning to wonder if these *guilty* ones have the most meat. They end up being the little splashes of color that decorate your brutalist, non-descript cubicle that your life can become when you're not paying attention. *IF** you're thoughtful, you start to find that your tastes are arguably *more* defined than the so-called critic's - that lived experience trumps the academic every time.

The silliness of critique is well-illustrated by Mr. Keating in his first interaction with students in *Dead Poets Society*.  <img src="\img\keating.jpeg" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 300px;"> Young Neil Perry reads from the introduction to their poetry text as written by Dr. J. Evan Pritchard, PhD. He explains that we can evaluate a poem by scoring it on a Cartesian plane with *Perfection* on the $x$-axis and *Importance* on the $y$. We can thus determine the poem's greatness by it's total area. Mr. Keating calls this "excrement" and orders these buttoned-up little foundlings to tear out the page &mdash; no, tear out the whole introduction &mdash; from their pristine text books which they are likely to be fined for come the end of the semester.

The lesson here is more layered than we give it credit for. This is a lesson in interrogating the sacred. Both the completeness of the book and the authority of the voice therein are categorically dismissed. Such a lesson is a risky one to give to young men (I can only speak as having been a young man though I expect it is not limited by sex or gender). Now, authority is going to come under question eventually. At least one of a group will be stricken awake one late night when they say to themselves *wait a second, why are we doing this? why is that even a thing?* I specify the *one* because it isn't always clear that you're abiding by totally arbitrary rules and conventions and there is little likelihood that someone is going to point it out to you.
Even in middle age, I find myself wandering around just doing stuff I'm supposed to. A couple of years ago, I was (uncharacteristically) heling my wife make the bed. I asked her how she knew which direction the fitted sheet went on the bed. She said there was a little tag in one of the corners and that she put that one in the top left. Exasperated by the notion that it involved investigation at all, I quipped "Why don't we just take a marker and little stick figures of us lying in the bed &mdash; I mean they're our *fucking* sheets." We laughed a little more than normal people would but, *yes, they ARE our goddamn sheets and we can do whatever we want to them*. This is not insignificant: two people that have made a life together whose course has been not insignificantly guided by the precept "go fuck yourself" when it comes to an authority flex.

We're talking about taste and subjectivity.  <img src="\img\daveMatthews.jpg" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 150px;">I'll start with this: I don't want to like the Dave Matthews Band &mdash; it doesn't gel with my whole schtick. However, I went to college on the Fall of 1994 in the SouthEast US. You could not walk by a dorm without "Ants Marching" blaring at different parts of the song from several windows. The next two years would be the same with Hootie and the Blowfish and Rusted Root and, often with the cooler hippies, Widespread Panic. I radicalized myself as a punk in like 1991. I heard Social Distortion while I was hanging out with my cousin and we got into a whole thing. The next day, <img src="\img\c-n-b.webp" style="float: right; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px; max-width: 200px;"> I picked up copies of Minor Threat *Out of Step*, the Misfits *Legacy of Brutality*, and Black Flag *Damaged* and committed to the bit. I had set foot in Crash-n-Burn (See {}) and the smell of the leather boots and silk-screen solvents changed my DNA. I leaned in and went for foundational stuff and became obsessed with the Sex Pistols and the Dead Kennedys. This is all to say that I was not about to allow myself to listen the whimsy of a South African hippy with an acoustic guitar. There were concessions made for the sake of getting girls like the Indigo Girls and Ani DIfranco &mdash; though she is as punk as they come so it wasn't difficult. And I'm not gonna lie, *Send Me On My Way* and that Blues Traveler song are fucking catchy &mdash;; I loved them. They were *in sutu* guilty pleasures. Now, 30+ years later, I have no control over the fact that Dave Matthews triggers a sense of fond nostalgia.
