Guilty Pleasures

We cannot control that which we hold dear
August 13, 2025 · 8 min · J. McMullen

The blanket acceptance of using the term ‘guilty pleasure’ to excuse our indulgence of things that aren’t collectively considered cool, classy, or otherwise justifiable is an efficient indicator of how necessary these transgressions of taste are. The longer you’re alive, or, rather, the more experiences you cobble together, the less control you have over the things that hold meaning for you and, thus, become indulgences. Only from the safe, sterile perch of the critic’s desk can one maintain a proper set of standards for art and food and music or anything else. As I get older, though, I am beginning to wonder if these guilty ones have the most meat. They end up being the little splashes of color that decorate your brutalist, non-descript cubicle that your life can become when you’re not paying attention. IF you’re thoughtful, you start to find that your tastes are arguably more defined than the so-called critic’s - that lived experience trumps the academic every time.

The silliness of critique is well-illustrated by Mr. Keating in his first interaction with students in Dead Poets Society. Young Neil Perry reads from the introduction to their poetry text as written by Dr. J. Evan Pritchard, PhD. He explains that we can evaluate a poem by scoring it on a Cartesian plane with Perfection on the xx-axis and Importance on the yy. We can thus determine the poem’s greatness by it’s total area. Mr. Keating calls this “excrement” and orders these buttoned-up little foundlings to tear out the page — no, tear out the whole introduction — from their pristine text books which they are likely to be fined for come the end of the semester.

The lesson here is more layered than we give it credit for. This is a lesson in interrogating the sacred. Both the completeness of the book and the authority of the voice therein are categorically dismissed. Such a lesson is a risky one to give to young men (I can only speak as having been a young man though I expect it is not limited by sex or gender). Now, authority is going to come under question eventually. At least one of a group will be stricken awake one late night when they say to themselves wait a second, why are we doing this? why is that even a thing? I specify the one because it isn’t always clear that you’re abiding by totally arbitrary rules and conventions and there is little likelihood that someone is going to point it out to you. Even in middle age, I find myself wandering around just doing stuff I’m supposed to. A couple of years ago, I was (uncharacteristically) heling my wife make the bed. I asked her how she knew which direction the fitted sheet went on the bed. She said there was a little tag in one of the corners and that she put that one in the top left. Exasperated by the notion that it involved investigation at all, I quipped “Why don’t we just take a marker and little stick figures of us lying in the bed — I mean they’re our fucking sheets.” We laughed a little more than normal people would but, yes, they ARE our goddamn sheets and we can do whatever we want to them. This is not insignificant: two people that have made a life together whose course has been not insignificantly guided by the precept “go fuck yourself” when it comes to an authority flex.

Of course, a lot of things we’re supposed to do we’re supposed to do. The idea that just because something is a rule or convention, it is bad is moronic. This is where the youth run into trouble. You can see {placeholder} for more on rules and arbitrary authority. We’re talking about taste and subjectivity. I’ll start with this: I don’t want to like the Dave Matthews Band — it doesn’t gel with my whole schtick. However, I went to college on the Fall of 1994 in the SouthEast US. You could not walk by a dorm without “Ants Marching” blaring at different parts of the song from several windows. The next two years would be the same with Hootie and the Blowfish and Rusted Root and, often, with the cooler hippies, Widespread Panic.

I radicalized myself as a punk in like 1991. I heard Social Distortion while I was hanging out with my cousin and we got into a whole thing. The next day, I picked up copies of Minor Threat Out of Step, the Misfits Legacy of Brutality, and Black Flag Damaged and committed to the bit. I had set foot in Crash-n-Burn (See {}) and the smell of the leather boots and silk-screen solvents changed my DNA. I leaned in and went for foundational stuff and became obsessed with the Sex Pistols and the Dead Kennedys. This is all to say that I was not about to allow myself to listen the whimsy of a South African hippy with an acoustic guitar. There were concessions made for the sake of getting girls like the Indigo Girls and Ani DIfranco — though she is as punk as they come so it wasn’t difficult. And I’m not gonna lie, Send Me On My Way and that Blues Traveler song are fucking catchy — I loved them. They were in sutu guilty pleasures. See { } for more about the creation of identity and having to make concessions.

Now, 30+ years later, I have no control over the fact that Dave Matthews triggers a sense of fond nostalgia. One night, those many years ago, I tried to control this effect by putting a James Taylor song on repeat for the entirety of a mushroom- and wine-fueled evening. To a degree it worked, in that I know what song it was but it doesn’t “take me there” like I was expecting. Perhaps this has more to do with the chemistry. Similarly, I have no control over all the terrible music of the late 90’s that was incessant in the restaurant I worked at, the songs on the radio when I was mostly drunk between classes at USF, which do, in fact, take me there. Some movies are great at this. Lost in Translation overrides any other memory I had of *Just Like Honey; Fight Club own Where is My Mind; Resevoir Dogs and Stuck in the Middle=.

But music is obviously subjective and personal. What about art and food and movies? I have a very priveleged palate that has been around the block but, if I’m honest, the best foods are pizza, hot dogs, chicken tenders, and vanilla ice cream.

If you like Grease (1), then you have no business trashing Grease 2. Michelle Pfieffer is smokin’ hot and Score Tonight is a banger. Drowning Mona is one of the best comedies ever. Skinamarink is self-indulgent garbage. I will stick by all these statements and be unmoved by your reasons otherwise. However, the approached to defending them are wildly different. Grease 2 is a musical — they’re all silly and criticizing them is a fool’s errand. You can like the people, you can like the songs, you can like the story — there are depressingly few that are going to nail all three. Drowning Mona? Banger cast, dark and hilariously absurd, and littered with one-liners that alone justify it. Skinamarink could have been 45 minutes long and it would still be asking a lot of me. I love horror and we’ve had some great arthouse horror, especially in the last decade, but Skinamarink and Backrooms ain’t it.

FOR ME.

One the most useful things that came out of the lived-experience/woke/radical subjectviity of the last fifteen years is that has allowed for things to exist and be marketable and that you are exposed to that aren’t for you. Maybe there is something to sitting uncomfortably for 2+ hours of dark and ominous and otherwise disconcerting camera play. That makes sense. And some people can, could, and probably did benefit from it. I do think I’d like to see it what would happen if it were projected on the lid of a floating sensory dep tank. I be that would fuck me up real good.

The point is that it probably wasn’t made for me or I haven’t had the experience that makes its point salient for me. And that’s exactly why we have to be careful about athoritative claims. You can’t tell me that I have to like this movie when it is clear that it has a very specific experience that it wants to tap into. You don’t have to like Grease 2, but if you’re going to bat for Grease (1), then you don’t have a leg to stand on.

I love stories about the Gulag and imprisonment — I am totally enthralled with the atmosphere. But I have also written quite a bit about the isolation that is a fundamental part of addiction so it speaks to me in a very specific way. However, I do not think it is a necessarily fundamental human experience. And that is the crux of all of this.

When I was in my twenties, we used to go to the Tampa Theater for their Summer Classic Movie Series. Booze, friends, and a matinee after brunch was pretty good living. The overture was played live of the Mighty Wurlitzer organ before the shows and the shows were generally unequivocal classics. Some were good by association — like Spellbound, but most you’d stick by: Psycho, Casablanca, Citizen Kane.
